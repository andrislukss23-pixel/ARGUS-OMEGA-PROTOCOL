1. Executive statement

ARGUS Ω is an adversarial research and reasoning architecture designed to prevent a claim from gaining more confidence, authority, scope, or apparent certainty than its evidence can support.

Its governing principle is:

«REALITY OUTRANKS THE MODEL.»

ARGUS does not assume that a source is correct because it is authoritative.

ARGUS does not assume that a model is correct because it predicts well.

ARGUS does not assume that multiple validators are independent because they have different names.

ARGUS does not assume that cryptographic authentication makes a statement true.

ARGUS does not assume that a successful repair establishes that all relevant vulnerabilities have been eliminated.

ARGUS does not assume that internal adversarial success constitutes external scientific validation.

ARGUS does not assume that its hypothesis space contains reality.

ARGUS does not assume that ARGUS itself deserves epistemic privilege.

The protocol therefore attacks not only a claim, but also:

- the definitions used to express it;
- the evidence used to support it;
- the provenance of that evidence;
- the causal interpretation;
- the measurement process;
- the independence assumptions;
- the dependency graph;
- the evaluator;
- the validator;
- the benchmark;
- the repair;
- the governance mechanism;
- the conditions under which evidence was selected;
- the possibility of hidden common causes;
- the possibility of unmeasured dependencies;
- and the possibility that the entire model class is wrong.

The intended output is not artificial certainty.

The intended output is the strongest conclusion that survives without crossing the licensed evidence boundary.

Where evidence cannot discriminate, ARGUS must return:

"UNRESOLVED"

or:

"MODEL_GAP"

rather than manufacture certainty.

4. Core architecture

4.1 HIRAKU — Hypothesis Expansion

HIRAKU expands the hypothesis space before selection.

Purpose:

- generate competing explanations;
- prevent first-story lock-in;
- expose omitted variables;
- expose alternative causal structures;
- identify latent common causes;
- generate rival mechanisms;
- construct countermodels;
- look for observationally equivalent worlds;
- search for explanations outside the preferred conceptual vocabulary;
- force distinctive predictions wherever possible.

HIRAKU exists because comparing only:

preferred hypothesis
vs.
null hypothesis

can leave entire classes of alternatives invisible.

The correct question is not merely:

«Does hypothesis H fit?»

It is also:

«What other hypotheses could produce the same observation?»

and:

«What observation would distinguish them?»

23. T1423 mathematical counterexamples

M01 — Observational equivalence does not identify causal effect

Construct three zero-mean Gaussian causal models:

World A: common cause
World B: X → Y
World C: Y → X

All can be constructed with covariance:

[[2, 1],
 [1, 2]]

while producing different interventional effects.

For "do(X=1)":

E[Y | do(X=1)] =
  0
  1/2
  0

depending on the causal world.

Licensed conclusion:

«The observational law alone does not universally identify the causal effect across these admissible models.»

---

M02 — Collider/selection-induced negative association

Begin with independent fair binary variables:

X ⟂ Y

Retain only cases where:

X OR Y = 1

The original population is independent.

Inside the selected population:

correlation = -1/2

Licensed conclusion:

«Selected association does not establish X→Y or shared production ancestry.»

---

M03 — Selection can create perfect positive association

Again begin with independent fair binary variables.

Select:

X = Y

The selected sample can exhibit:

correlation = +1

even though the underlying variables were independent in the generating population.

Licensed conclusion:

«Even perfect selected association is not sufficient evidence for the claimed causal/provenance relationship.»

---

M04 — Common trend creates extreme correlation

Construct independent signs:

T, A, B

and define:

X = 10T + A
Y = 10T + B

Then:

correlation = 100/101
            ≈ 0.990099

without requiring a direct causal edge between X and Y.

Licensed conclusion:

«Very high telemetry correlation can arise from a common trend and does not by itself establish a direct dependency.»

---

M05 — Zero correlation does not certify independence

Let:

X ∈ {-1, 0, 1}
Y = X²

Then covariance can be zero while Y is deterministically dependent on X.

Licensed conclusion:

«Zero correlation is not a certificate of independence.»

---

M06 — Pooled independence can conceal regime dependence

Construct independent signs:

H, X

with:

Y = H X

The pooled variables may appear independent while regime-specific correlations differ:

H = -1 → correlation -1
H = +1 → correlation +1

Licensed conclusion:

«Pooled independence does not establish independence across all regimes.»

No non-zero population-average causal effect is implied by this example.

24. T1423 decisive indistinguishable-world construction

Consider:

World C

X = U + eX
Y = U + eY

where:

U, eX, eY

are independent standard Gaussian variables.

World D

Let:

Var(X) = 2
Y = (1/2)X + η
Var(η) = 3/2

with η independent of X.

Both systems are zero-mean jointly Gaussian and have the same covariance matrix.

Therefore they possess the same full observational distribution.

Yet under intervention:

do(X=1)

the expected value of Y differs:

World C: E[Y | do(X=1)] = 0
World D: E[Y | do(X=1)] = 1/2

Therefore a universal method receiving only:

identical observations
+
identical unverified metadata

cannot guarantee the correct causal answer in both worlds.

This does not imply causal effects are never identifiable.

It establishes the narrower point:

«Identification requires assumptions, interventions, structure, or evidence sufficient to distinguish otherwise observationally equivalent worlds.»
